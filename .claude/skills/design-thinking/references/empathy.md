# Empathy — Full Reference

## Core Truth

> "Empathy is not asking users what they want — it's experiencing what they experience."

The most important question is never **"What do they need?"**
It's **"What are they afraid of?"**

Fear drives inaction far more than lack of information or capability.

---

## The 3 Layers of Empathy

### Layer 1 — Observe

**What:** Watch real behavior without interfering or asking.
**Why:** The gap between what people say and what they do is where insight lives.

**How:**

- Watch where users pause, backtrack, or abandon
- Notice what they do repeatedly vs. what they say they want
- Shadow them in their real environment, not a lab

**The Airbnb Example:**
Founders stayed in Airbnb listings themselves instead of sending surveys.
They discovered the photos were terrible — something no survey would have surfaced.
Revenue doubled in one month after hiring photographers.

**Output:** A raw list of observed behaviors — what they actually do, step by step.

---

### Layer 2 — Engage

**What:** Ask questions designed to surface real motivations, not surface opinions.
**Why:** First answers are always rationalizations. The third answer is usually the truth.

**The Wrong Questions:**

```
❌ "Do you like this feature?"
❌ "Would you use this if we built it?"
❌ "What do you think about X?"
```

These invite opinion, not insight. People will tell you what they think you want to hear.

**The Right Questions:**

```
✅ "Walk me through the last time you had this problem."
✅ "What did you do when that happened?"
✅ "Why didn't you just [obvious solution]?"
✅ "What were you afraid might go wrong?"
```

**The "Why?" Chain:**
Ask "Why?" to every answer at least 3 times.

```
"I don't set up more because I'm busy."
→ Why? "Too many things going on."
→ Why can't you spare 5 minutes? "Honestly, I don't know what I'd get from it."
← THIS is the real reason — not busyness, but unclear ROI.
```

---

### Layer 3 — Immerse

**What:** Put yourself fully in the user's situation — not metaphorically, literally.
**Why:** Reading about a problem is different from feeling it.

**The OXO Good Grips Example:**
Sam Farber wore thick gloves to feel his arthritis-suffering wife's pain firsthand.
He discovered handles were too thin, too rigid, too slippery.
OXO Good Grips became the world's bestselling kitchen tool line.
He didn't ask her what she needed. He felt it himself.

**How to Immerse:**

- Complete the user's entire workflow yourself, from scratch
- Use only what they have access to
- Notice every point of friction, confusion, or hesitation
- Write down every moment you felt stuck or uncertain

## Empathy Map — Full Guide

### The 4 Quadrants

**THINK & FEEL** — What's going on inside their head

- Deep worries they wouldn't say out loud
- Unstated expectations and assumptions
- What success means to them emotionally
- What failure would feel like

_Key question: "What keeps them up at night about this?"_

**HEAR** — What their environment is telling them

- What their boss, colleagues, or customers say
- Industry noise and trends they're exposed to
- Social pressure and comparison points

_Key question: "Who influences their decisions and what are those people saying?"_

**SEE** — What they observe around them

- What competitors are doing
- What solutions others are using
- What "normal" looks like in their context

_Key question: "What does the world look like from where they stand?"_

**SAY & DO** — The observable surface

- What they actually say in meetings, support tickets, reviews
- What they physically do — clicks, workarounds, habits
- ⚠️ When SAY ≠ DO: this gap is the most valuable insight on the entire map

_Key question: "What do I observe them doing vs. what do they claim to do?"_

### PAIN (bottom left)

The real fear — not the surface complaint.

Always distinguish:

```
Surface complaint: "It's too complicated"
Real fear: "If I mess this up, I'll look incompetent in front of my team"
```

_Ask: "What is the worst thing that could happen if this goes wrong?"_

### GAIN (bottom right)

The real desired outcome — not the feature they're requesting.

Always distinguish:

```
Requested feature: "Make the dashboard faster"
Real outcome: "I need to feel confident in meetings when showing data"
```

_Ask: "If this worked perfectly, what would their life look like differently?"_

## Jobs-to-be-Done

"Customers don't buy products. They hire them to do a specific job."
— Clayton Christensen

### The Framework

Every product or feature is "hired" to make progress in a specific situation.

```
When [SITUATION] arises,
I want to [MOTIVATION],
so I can [EXPECTED OUTCOME].
```

### Why It Matters

Two users can use the same product for completely different jobs.
If you design for the wrong job, the product fails — even if it's technically excellent.

### Finding the Job

Ask: "What were you trying to accomplish when you first started looking for a solution like this?"
Not: "Why did you buy our product?"

### Jobs Have Functional and Emotional Dimensions

```
Functional job: "Answer customer questions automatically"
Emotional job:  "Stop feeling guilty about leaving customers unanswered at night"
Social job:     "Look like a professional, tech-forward business"
```

The emotional job is usually the real hiring trigger.

## Empathy-Driven Communication

### Pitching with Empathy

Lead with their pain — not your solution.

```
❌ "Our product has AI that automatically responds 24/7."

✅ "How many hours does your team spend every day answering
    the same questions over and over? What would it mean
    to get that time back?"
```

### The 3-Step Formula

1. **Name their pain** (not your feature)
2. **Agitate:** "If this doesn't change, what happens next quarter?"
3. **Solve:** "Here's exactly how we address that."

### Storytelling for Empathy

Great stories that create empathy have 4 elements (Lewrick):

1. **Emotionally real situation** — A scenario the listener recognizes
2. **Relatable character** — Not a persona, a person
3. **Conflict leading to change** — The tension that demands a solution
4. **Moral or takeaway** — What this means for them

## Common Empathy Mistakes

| Mistake                                    | Why It Fails                           | Correct Approach                                 |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Asking "Do you like this?"                 | Invites social desirability bias       | "Walk me through last time you had this problem" |
| Running focus groups for behavior insights | Group dynamics suppress honest answers | 1-on-1 observation in real context               |
| Stopping at the stated problem             | Always a symptom                       | Ask "Why?" until you can't anymore               |
| Assuming all users have the same job       | Different users, different motivations | Map each segment's job separately                |
| Using surveys to understand emotions       | Emotions don't survive survey format   | Immersive observation and conversation           |
| Building for your most vocal user          | Loudest ≠ most representative          | Find the silent majority                         |
